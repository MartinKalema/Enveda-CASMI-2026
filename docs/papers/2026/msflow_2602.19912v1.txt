                                                                                                 A BSTRACT
                                                     Mass spectrometry is a powerful and widely used tool for identifying molecular structures due to its
                                                     sensitivity and ability to profile complex samples. However, translating spectra into full molecular
                                                     structures is a difficult, under-defined inverse problem. Overcoming this problem is crucial for
                                                     enabling biological insight, discovering new metabolites, and advancing chemical research across
                                                     multiple fields. To this end, we develop MSFlow, a two-stage encoder-decoder flow-matching
                                                     generative model that achieves state-of-the-art performance on the structure elucidation task for small
                                                     molecules. In the first stage, we adopt a formula-restricted transformer model for encoding mass
                                                     spectra into a continuous and chemically informative embedding space, while in the second stage we
                                                     train a decoder flow matching model to reconstruct molecules from latent embeddings of mass spectra.
                                                     We present ablation studies demonstrating the importance of using information-preserving molecular
                                                     descriptors for encoding mass spectra and motivate the use of our discrete flow-based decoder. Our
                                                     rigorous evaluation demonstrates that MSFlow can accurately translate up to 45% of molecular mass
                                                     spectra into their corresponding molecular representations — an improvement up to fourteen-fold
                                                     over the current state-of-the-art. A trained version of MSFlow is made publicly available on GitHub
                                                     for non-commercial users.

                                         1       Introduction
                                         Mass spectrometry (MS) is a vital analytical method used in chemistry and molecular biology for measuring the
                                         mass-to-charge (m/z) ratio of target compounds. Mass spectrometers provide sensitive, high-throughput, and precise
                                         measurements that aid in identifying unknown compounds by determining their molecular weight. A mass spectrometer
                                         contains three main components: an ionization source, a mass analyzer, and an ion detection system. First, molecules
                                         are converted to gas-phase ions that can be manipulated via external electrical and magnetic fields. Then, ions are
                                         separated according to their respective m/z ratios. Finally, the output of the mass analyzer is forwarded to a data system
                                         where m/z ratios are stored along with their relative abundances. The result is a mass spectrum, which plots m/z ratios
                                         on the x-axis against relative abundances (intensities) on the y-axis. MS is widely used for the structural characterization
                                         of small molecules, natural products, and metabolites, enabling researchers to probe complex biological and chemical
                                         systems. In the rapidly expanding field of metabolomics, tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS or MS2) plays a central
                                         role in elucidating complex molecular structures at scale. Tandem mass spectrometry is a two-stage analytical technique
                                         used to obtain detailed structural information. In the first stage (MS1), all ions in the sample are measured, and a
                                         specific ion of interest—called the precursor ion—is isolated, typically based on having the highest intensity. The
                                         precursor ion then undergoes fragmentation, usually by collision-induced dissociation, and the resulting fragment ions
                                         are analyzed in the second stage (MS2) to determine their masses. As shown in Figure 1, an MS2 spectrum displays

Despite the rich information provided by MS/MS, converting fragmentation spectra into complete molecular structures
remains a fundamental inverse problem. Due to the ionization and fragmentation processes in the mass spectrometer,
identifying a unique structure from a single spectrum is a hard inverse problem. The task is inherently difficult because
the mapping from molecular structure to fragmentation spectrum is one-to-many: A certain molecular structure can be
fragmented in different paths, producing highly similar spectra. Consequently, a large proportion of molecules detected
in untargeted MS-based metabolomics studies remain unknown and missing in virtual chemical libraries, forming the
so-called “dark matter in metabolomics” [1]. Often up to 87% of observed spectra in large public MS/MS databases
remain unannotated [2, 1]. Moreover, Huber et al. [3] report that generally only 10% of reproducible spectral features
can be identified even in well-studied matrices such as human serum. Solving this inverse problem is essential for
advancing metabolomics and chemical biology, as it enables the annotation of unknown metabolites, the discovery of
novel molecules, and the accurate interpretation of biochemical pathways.

2   Related Work
Before de novo structure generation, researchers have addressed the forward problem of predicting spectra from
molecular structures using deep learning methods, including multilayer perceptrons[4], graph neural networks, and
Transformers [5, 6, 7]. Further forward models have leveraged molecular (sub)formulas through graph-based approaches
[8, 9, 10]. Complementary to these prediction-based methods, other work has focused on learning spectral similarity
measures for retrieval and clustering. Spectral representation learning methods [11, 3] learn embeddings to predict
structural similarities directly from spectral pairs, enabling molecular networking and compound clustering without
requiring known structures.
The problem of molecular structure elucidation from mass spectra has recently gained interest in computational
chemistry as an alternative to the traditional (enhanced) database search. More specifically, different machine learning
methods have been used to solve this inverse problem [12]. The task can be described by taking the mass spectrum
as input and predicting a representation of the molecular structure as output. Several molecular representations have
been developed, including fingerprints, graph-based and textual representations, such as the Simplified Molecular
Input Line Entry System (SMILES)[13] or the more recent Sequential Attachment-based Fragment Embedding
(SAFE)[14]. These representations have enabled the development of different MS-to-structure machine learning
approaches [15][16][17][10]. Recent state-of-the-art approaches frame the task as the de novo generation of molecules
from mass spectra. These methods can be classified mainly into autoregressive and diffusion-based approaches. Some
prior studies [16] [17] used language models to generate SMILES from tokenized input (m/z) and intensity pairs.
Tokenized (m/z, intensity) representations fail to capture the permutation-invariant nature of mass spectra and cannot
enforce chemical formula constraints. Furthermore, autoregressive models suffer from two key limitations: they can get
stuck in suboptimal local paths, necessitating beam search for adequate chemical space exploration, and their slower
inference compared to flow-based models makes them impractical for large spectral datasets. Also, SMILES-based
approaches depict molecular substructures non-sequentially, which imposes a challenge for de novo generation and
highlights the need to use better textual molecular representation.
Diffusion models [18, 19, 20] emerged as a powerful method for different generative tasks. Building on prior work that
converts mass spectra into circular fingerprints using MIST [21], Bohde et al. [22] employed a diffusion decoder to
reconstruct chemical compounds from these fingerprints. However, a key limitation of this approach is the irreversible
loss of information when encoding mass spectra into binary fingerprints—different molecules often map to identical

3     Methods

We formulate the task as follows: We represent structure-spectrum pairs as (X, S), where X denotes the SAFE
representation of a molecule and S its corresponding spectrum. As the task of predicting a molecule X given a spectrum
S is under-defined, the goal of conditional generation is to generate k structures that most closely match the input
spectra.
Conceptually, MSFlow tackles the molecular structure elucidation problem in two stages. In the first stage, mass
spectra S are encoded into intermediate descriptors Y . In this stage, a labeled dataset of (spectrum, descriptor) pairs is
constructed and used to train a MIST encoder [21] as shown in Figure 2A. In the second stage, a flow-based generative
decoder uses these descriptors Y as conditioning to map back to molecular representations X as visualized in Figure
2B.


3.1        SAFE molecule representation

Sequential Attachment-based Fragment Embedding (SAFE) is a novel line notation for molecular representation [25].
SAFE represents molecules as an unordered sequence of fragments or substructures. Molecules are decomposed into
fragments using the BRICS algorithm [26]. Fragments within the SAFE representation are concatenated using a dot
token (.) while explicitly retaining their attachment points. This formulation ensures permutation invariance across
fragments: reordering fragments in a SAFE string preserves the identical molecular structure. The main difference from
the commonly used SMILES notation is that the arrangement of SMILES tokens corresponding to the same molecular
fragment is consecutive.

3.2   Mass spectrum encoding

To leverage mass spectra in machine learning models, an effective numerical representation is essential. Binning the
spectra can transform it into a fixed-size vector by aggregating intensities (e.g., via summation) within each bin. Ideally,
one can directly use this vectorized spectrum as an input condition for guided de novo molecular generation. However,
a key practical limitation is the scarcity of labeled mass spectrometry data for training generative models that map
spectra directly to molecules.
In contrast, vast paired structure-descriptor datasets can be readily generated from virtual chemical libraries, given the
infinite diversity of possible molecules. This enables pretraining of a decoder model on large-scale data, benefiting
from established scaling laws, at the cost of potential information loss during the spectrum-to-representation S → Y
encoding step. The transformation function F : S → Y should preserve as much chemical information as possible and
be ideally bijective.
In our work, we adopt the continuous molecular descriptors introduced by Winter et al. [24], which have been
successfully used as molecular representations in a range of chemoinformatics applications [27, 28]. Winter et al. [24]
obtain these descriptors from an autoencoder trained to translate input SMILES strings into their canonical SMILES
equivalents, with a 512-dimensional bottleneck layer; on top of supporting this autoencoding/translation objective, the
resulting bottleneck embeddings (termed CDDD) provide a compact and transferable representation for downstream
tasks. Compared to models relying on molecular fingerprints, these continuous descriptors are more expressive and
chemically informative and lead to less information loss.

3.3   Discrete flow matching decoder

Discrete flow matching (DFM) models employ a continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) over sequences of discrete
tokens [29, 30]. In a CTMC, each token alternates between staying in its current state i and transitioning to another state
j over infinitesimal time intervals. We apply DFM to generate molecular SAFE sequences X ∈ V L , where V denotes
the vocabulary of tokens (e.g., atoms, bonds, branch indicators), L is the maximum sequence length, and K = |V | is
the vocabulary size.

we describe the dynamics for a single token position. Transitions from state i to state j are governed by a parameterized

3.4       Datasets

For encoder training and model evaluation, we use NPLIB1 (CANOPUS) [38], or the MassSpecGym [39] dataset,
according to the defined training-val-test splits. These public datasets offer labeled spectrum-structure pairs (S, X)
and are described in the following two paragraphs. The distribution of molecular properties across different splits for
both benchmarks is shown in Figure 3, revealing that CANOPUS provides an in-distribution evaluation scenario with
balanced train-test splits, while MSG constitutes a challenging out-of-distribution benchmark with test molecules being
substantially larger and more flexible than those in the training set.


NPLIB1 (CANOPUS) The dataset contains MS/MS spectra paired with molecular structures and compound class
annotations derived from ClassyFire taxonomy to ensure an even distribution of chemical classes[39]. The dataset
employs standard splitting based on 2D InChIKey hashes to ensure molecules with identical planar structures do not
appear across different data splits. The dataset contains around 8K spectra of roughly 7K unique molecules.

MassSpecGym MassSpecGym is a rigorously curated benchmark dataset designed to evaluate model generalization
to structurally novel molecules. Unlike conventional splitting methods based on 2D InChIKey hashes, MassSpecGym
implements a structure-aware splitting strategy that guarantees a minimum chemical bond edit distance (MCES distance)

Decoder training set For decoder training, we curate a large-scale corpus from publicly available chemical databases.
Specifically, the training set comprises molecules obtained by merging DSSTox [40], HMDB [41], COCONUT [42],
and MOSES [43]. We also add the small training sets of molecules present in NPLIB1 and MassSpecGym to form a
total set of approximately 2.8M molecules for decoder training using the setup described in Section 3.3. To ensure a
fair assessment of generalization and novelty, we exclude from this corpus any molecules that appear in the validation
or test splits of NPLIB1 (CANOPUS) or MassSpecGym so that evaluation is performed on structures unseen during

3.5   Model parameterization

We employ an encoder-decoder pipeline that decouples training of the spectrum encoder from the conditional molecular
decoder. The encoder builds on the MIST architecture [21] and maps each input spectrum to a continuous condition
vector Y in the space of CDDD embeddings, which is then provided to the flow-matching decoder as conditioning
(Figure 2B). The encoder takes (m/z, intensity) peak pairs as input. For each spectrum, it incorporates a predicted
molecular formula assignment produced by SIRIUS [44] and uses a set transformer to aggregate the peaks into a
fixed-length CDDD vector Y corresponding to the precursor ion.
The flow-based decoder is a BERT-like transformer trained with the discrete flow matching objective in Eq. (3).
Bidirectional self-attention provides global molecular context without relying on an autoregressive token order, while
non-autoregressive, parallel decoding further improves computational efficiency. Architectural and training details
are deferred to Appendix 3.3. To train the decoder for spectrum-to-structure elucidation, we precompute CDDD
embeddings for molecules in our dataset, yielding paired data (X, Y ), where X is the SAFE sequence and Y is the
corresponding CDDD embedding. We use these pairs to learn a conditional flow matching model pθ (X | Y ) that
samples SAFE sequences from descriptor-based conditions (Figure 2B) using classifier-free guidance. In this setup, in
each training batch, the CDDD embedding Y serves as a condition that is randomly dropped with a fixed probability.
This enables the model to learn both unconditional generation pθ (X) and conditional generation pθ (X | Y ). For
inference, these two components are combined with a controllable guidance scale for generating SAFE sequences. This
exposure to missing conditions during training prevents overfitting to ground truth CDDD conditions, which increases
the robustness of the model to potentially noisy and off-manifold predicted CDDD embeddings by the encoder at
inference.
At inference time, we apply the MIST encoder to a query spectrum to predict its CDDD embedding, which is then fed
to the flow-based decoder as conditioning to generate the corresponding molecular structure (Figure 2C).

3.6   Baselines and evaluation benchmarks

We evaluate our model, MSFlow, on two open-source benchmark datasets: NPLIB1 (CANOPUS) [38], and MassSpec-
Gym [39]. We follow the same train/validation/test splits used in DiffMS by Bohde et al. [22]. We compare our model
to different baseline methods that were re-implemented to be trained on the same datasets we used for fair evaluation.
As baselines, we include two models as decoders that reconstruct molecules from Morgan fingerprints. Neuraldecipher
[28], which uses an MLP to reconstruct molecules from ECFPs, meanwhile MSNovelist [15] utilizes a long short-term
memory (LSTM) network to decode fingerprints back to SMILES. These models are added as a decoding step to
Morgan fingerprints predicted by the MIST [21] encoder.
Spec2Mol [17] employs a two-stage training pipeline. First, a GRU-based autoencoder is trained to reconstruct SMILES
sequences, yielding a continuous latent space that captures molecular structure. Second, a CNN-based spectrum
encoder is trained to map input mass spectra into the same latent space by minimizing the distance to the SMILES
autoencoder’s latent representations of corresponding molecules. During inference, the spectrum encoder projects
spectra into the latent space, which is further decoded into candidate SMILES strings using only the SMILES decoder
from the autoencoder.
Another approach, MADGEN [45], introduces a two-stage generative setting. It relies on a diffusion-based generative
model to generate chemical structures from scaffolds. The accuracy of this approach is limited by the accuracy of
MS/MS spectrum-to-scaffold prediction.
A recent state-of-the-art model, DiffMS [22], utilized MIST[21] to convert mass spectra into circular molecular
fingerprints. As a second step, DiffMS uses a graph-based diffusion decoder, which was pretrained on vast fingerprint-
structure pairs from virtual chemical libraries.
MS-BART [46] is a BERT-based encoder-decoder model with unified tokenization for molecular fingerprints (4096-bit
ECPF) and SELFIES sequences. Like DiffMS, it utilized MIST to convert mass spectra to ECPF fingerprints. Training
comprises three stages: pretraining on 4 million simulated fingerprint-molecule pairs via multi-task denoising, finetuning
on experimental spectra to bridge the sim-to-real gap, and contrastive alignment through ranking loss to prioritize
structurally similar candidates based on Tanimoto similarity.

3.7   Evaluation metrics

For evaluating our model, we sample 100 molecules per spectrum. We rank the generated molecules based on their
frequencies. In alignment with the results of our baseline methods, we use three top-k metrics of de novo generation.
Top-k accuracy measures if the true molecule is in the top-k predictions of our model. Top-k maximum Tanimoto

similarity measures the structural similarity between the closest molecule in the top-k predictions and the true molecule.
We also use the graph edit distance developed by Kretschmer et al. [47] as a distance metric. This metric measures the

section as MCES (maximum common edge subgraph). All metrics are reported for k = 1, 10, where applicable.

MSFlow (conditioned on CDDD embeddings) consistently outperforms prior baselines on both NPLIB1 and MassSpec-
Gym across all reported metrics, as shown in Table 1. On NPLIB1, MSFlow achieves a Top-1 accuracy of 44.70%,
improving substantially over DiffMS (8.34%; ∼ 5.4×), while also increasing structural similarity (Top-1 Tanimoto 0.72
vs. 0.35) and reducing MCES (3.79 vs. 11.95). On the more challenging out-of-distribution MassSpecGym benchmark,
the gain is even more pronounced: MSFlow reaches 32.00% Top-1 accuracy compared to 2.30% for DiffMS (∼ 13.9×),
with corresponding improvements in Top-1 Tanimoto (0.69 vs. 0.28) and MCES (6.38 vs. 18.45). MSFlow further
attains strong Top-10 performance, reaching 58.53% on NPLIB1 and 42.53% on MassSpecGym.
To isolate the contribution of our SAFE-based flow matching decoder relative to ECFP-conditioned decoders, we train
an ablated variant MSFlow (ECFP) that conditions on Morgan fingerprints, analogous to DiffMS. MSFlow (ECFP)
exceeds DiffMS on all metrics for both datasets (e.g., NPLIB1 Top-1 accuracy 12.95% vs. 8.34%; MassSpecGym
Top-1 accuracy 5.17% vs. 2.30%), providing empirical support for the superiority of our decoder design.
Finally, MSFlow (CDDD) improves upon the original CDDD decoder baseline, increasing Top-1 accuracy from 40.97%
to 44.70% on NPLIB1 and from 24.10% to 32.00% on MassSpecGym. Comparing conditioning representations
within the same decoder further highlights the benefit of CDDD-based conditioning over ECFP-based conditioning:

  pathways in mass spectrometry. As a result, the same molecular structure may yield diverse fragmentation spectra.

This variability introduces significant complexity, thereby making the inverse problem of deducing molecular structure
from spectral data more challenging. To investigate the effect of the molecular flexibility on our model, we stratify
molecules in the MassSpecGym test set by the number of rotatable bonds and compute the top-1 accuracy for each
bin, as shown in Figure 4D. Since reconstruction accuracy degrades with increasing molecular size, we restrict our
flexibility analysis to molecules consisting of 20-25 heavy atoms. We can observe that MSFlow performs consistently
across rigid (4> rotatable bonds) and highly flexible molecules (rotatable bonds >= 7), indicating its robustness with
respect to molecular flexibility.
In the MassSpecGym dataset, each unique molecular structure is associated with a highly variable number of spectra,
ranging from 1 up to 383 spectra (median 3) for the test set. Prior benchmarking studies have reported only the average
prediction accuracy over all spectra (see Table 1), but this metric can be misleading because molecules represented by
many spectra disproportionately influence the overall score. For example, a method that correctly predicts only the
single molecule with the most spectra achieves 2.24% accuracy, whereas its accuracy measured per unique molecule
would be only 0.03%. To provide a more balanced evaluation, we additionally report the mean accuracy computed per
unique molecule. Under this metric, the CDDD decoder and MSFlow-CDDD achieve mean accuracies of 29.1% and
29.6%, respectively, while MSFlow-ECFP performs substantially lower at 0.9%.
Figure 5 visualizes success and failure modes among the top-1 and top-10 predictions of our model. The leftmost
example demonstrates successful reconstruction in the top-1 prediction, achieving perfect Tanimoto similarity (1.00)
and MCES distance (0). The second example fails in top-1 but succeeds in top-10, highlighting the benefit of generating
multiple candidates. The three rightmost examples illustrate failure cases where the model converges to structurally
incorrect scaffolds. This behavior can be attributed to the many-to-one mapping in the training data, where multiple
distinct molecules can produce similar spectra, diluting the conditioning embeddings. Notably, even in failure cases,
top-10 predictions often yield higher structural similarity than top-1, suggesting that relevant structural motifs are
present within the model’s hypothesis space but may not rank highest.

Finally, we analyze the ability of our model to predict certain functional groups. To conduct this analysis, we define a
comprehensive list of functional groups based on SMARTS patterns in RDKit. The list includes the following functional
groups: ester, amide, carboxylate, lactone, sulfoxide, sulfone, thioether, thiol, phosphate, phosphonate, nitro, azide,
nitrile, heteroaromatic, and polycyclic aromatic rings, as well as epoxide and peroxide groups. Each SMARTS pattern
is converted into a query molecule and matched against the ground truth molecule and the corresponding predicted
molecule using substructure searching; if the pattern is present, the associated functional group name is recorded in the
relevant set (Set1: Functional groups that exist in the query molecule; Set2: Functional groups that exist in the predicted

molecule). Then, we compute the number of intersections (true positives), the number of differences of groups existing
in the predicted molecule but not in the query molecule (false positives), and the number of differences of patterns that
exist in the query molecule but do not exist in the predicted molecule (false negatives). We repeat this procedure for
each (ground truth, predicted) molecule pair, computing precision, recall, and F1-score. Finally, we report average
scores across all pairs. Our model achieves good functional group prediction performance with an average precision of
0.868, a recall of 0.863, and an F1 score of 0.854 ± 0.298 on the MassSpecGym test set. This indicates that our model
can capture several existing functional groups with high accuracy, without predicting nonexistent ones.

Conclusions
In this work, we present MSFlow CDDD, an encoder-decoder approach for molecular structure elucidation from mass
spectrometry data. The encoder maps the input spectrum to a continuous embedding space (CDDD), and our discrete
flow-based decoder uses CDDD embeddings to reconstruct the corresponding molecular structure. Our experiments
demonstrate the superior performance of our model against previous state-of-the-art approaches across two common
de novo generation benchmarks. Despite the fact that our model’s performance degrades with increasingly larger
molecules, MSFlow is still able to generate similar molecules to target molecules on different benchmarks across
different molecular sizes. Our experiments highlight that the spectrum encoding stage constitutes the primary limitation,
as evidenced by the substantial performance gap between oracle CDDD descriptors and those derived from experimental
spectra. This observation motivates a future research direction for the development of more expressive spectrum
representation techniques.
